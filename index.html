People with ALS, brainstem stroke, or high spinal injury often keep full control of one thing: where they look. Commercial eye trackers turn that into speech, but they are priced as medical equipment and rationed like it. This project asks how much of that capability a laptop's front camera can recover, and measures the answer honestly.

01

Selection without a hand

A webcam knows where you are looking to within a few degrees, about the size of a golf ball at arm's length. That is far too coarse for a keyboard, so the board has at most nine large zones and letters are grouped. To choose one you rest your eyes on it. A ring fills. When it closes, the zone is selected and the board moves on.

Try it with the cursor. Real use replaces the cursor with your gaze or your head.

02

From camera to voice

Two pointing modes share the same pipeline. Gaze mode reads where the iris sits inside the eye. Head mode reads the angle of the face, which a webcam measures far more accurately, for people who can still move their neck.

Every piece of logic, from the least‑squares calibration to the dwell state machine, is a pure function with its own tests. The camera and the screen are the only two things that touch the outside world.

What it cannot do yet

  1. Gaze is coarse. A webcam cannot resolve where on a keyboard you are looking. Nine zones is the honest ceiling on a laptop; six on a phone.
  2. Light matters. A window behind the user, or a dim room, degrades tracking. The app warns, but cannot fix it.
  3. Not clinically validated. Testers so far are healthy volunteers. People with ALS have different eye movement, fatigue, and posture. Their results will differ.
  4. Small pilot. The numbers above come from a handful of sessions. They show the tool works and roughly how fast it is. They do not prove anything statistically.
